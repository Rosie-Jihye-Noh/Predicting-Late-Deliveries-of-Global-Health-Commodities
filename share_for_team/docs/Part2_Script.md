# Part 2 Script (Slides 4–8) — First draft

## Slide 4 (3:15–3:55)

Let's start with how the goods travel. At first glance, sea and air look the same: both miss the window about 11 times in 100.

But sea orders were given more time. Nine in ten sea lines had 71 days of buffer or more, compared with about half of air lines. Compare lines with the same buffer, and sea misses more. With a short buffer, 27 in 100 sea lines missed, versus 15 in 100 by air.

Why? Sea trips are longer and less predictable: about 76 days versus 13 by air, and more than one in five sea trips ran a month longer than usual.

## Slide 5 (3:55–4:35)

Route is one part. Another is who's responsible along the way.

Under FCA, the supplier hands the goods to the program's carrier. Under DDP, the supplier delivers all the way, customs included.

At the same buffer, DDP missed less: with a short buffer, 12 in 100 lines versus 20 for FCA. At the other end, when the receiving country handled customs instead of the program, the odds were about one and a half times higher.

So the more times an order changes hands, the more often it missed. One possible reason: every hand-off means re-syncing paperwork and schedules, and a tight buffer leaves no time for that.

## Slide 6 (4:35–5:25)

Where the order goes matters even more. In Tanzania, 22 in 100 lines missed. In Ethiopia, fewer than 3 in 100.

And it's not the shipping mode. By air, 20 in 100 Tanzania lines missed, versus 3 in 100 for Ethiopia.

The gap shows up before the goods even leave. For the same product, lab reagents by air, goods waited a median of 61 days to be picked up for Tanzania, versus 12 for Ethiopia. After pickup, trip times were similar.

One possible reason is import permits: if a permit runs late, goods may sit and wait. But we couldn't confirm this from our data, and Tanzania's rate swung a lot from year to year.

## Slide 7 (5:25–6:05)

What's being shipped matters too. Remember the HIV drugs from the start of our talk? They were actually among the most reliable: about 8 in 100 lines missed. For lab reagents it was 13 in 100, and for medical supplies and condoms about 16.

HIV drug suppliers ran late less often, 28 in 100 versus about half for lab and medical supplies, and their orders had more buffer. One possible reason: there are more than 200 WHO-approved HIV medicines and big buyers pool their orders, while lab reagents often need cold storage and have a short shelf life.

## Slide 8 (6:05–6:35)

Finally, option A from the poll: paperwork. USAID approval time had no link to missing the window. It's usually just one day, so there's little to see.

What did matter was the time between approval and sending the order to the supplier. Every extra 30 days raised the odds of missing by about 8%. One possible reason: slow orders are often the ones without a pre-arranged supplier, and those suppliers ran late more often.
